### @@54 — Glossaire OMS des *behavioural insights* ⭐
> Ask five public health teams what a "nudge" is, and you'll get 5 answers. Ask them what "behavioural insights" means, and you may get 8. That ambiguity has a cost! It slows collaboration across disciplines; it makes evidence hard to compare and aggregate. But mostly, it stalls the translation of behavioural sciences into policy and practice. So we did something unglamorous but, I think, quietly important: we agreed on the words! Built together with the UCL Centre for Behaviour Change through a Delphi process and shaped by our WHO Technical Advisory Group, WHO staff across all six regions, the glossary was developed with high engagement of end-users who told us which terms genuinely needed clarity.

### @@39 — Taxonomie éthique des interventions comportementales ⭐
> I argue that BPP's conceptual infrastructure is quietly broken. Terms like "nudge," "boost," "sludge," and "budge" are used inconsistently, blurring real differences in mechanism, normative intent, and policy impact. Concepts are epistemic tools. Just as Mendeleev's periodic table or Linnaeus's taxonomy didn't merely organize knowledge but enabled prediction and discovery, conceptual rigor in BPP could do the same for behavioral governance. To that end, I propose a working ethical taxonomy of behavioral interventions across six dimensions: **mechanism, epistemic foundation, locus of harm, normative logic, transparency/consent, and temporality.** Without this rigor, BPP risks rhetorical inflation. With it, the field can advance as a scientifically grounded and ethically responsible approach to governance.

- https://academic.oup.com/policyandsociety/advance-article/doi/10.1093/polsoc/puag011/8676563

**→ Le pendant critique de @@54.** Là où l'OMS harmonise, ce papier montre que l'harmonisation masque des différences de *mécanisme* et d'*intention normative*. Les six dimensions proposées sont une grille utilisable telle quelle pour organiser la sous-section « différentes approches ».

### @@32 — L'angle mort géographique de la *behavioral public policy*
> Behavioral public policy has a blind spot — and it's time we call it out. For over a decade, governments like the UK and the US have championed behavioral insights as a smarter way to design policy. The field now presents itself as mature, evidence-based, and globally relevant. But in reality? It's still heavily concentrated in a narrow set of countries — and too often confined to small, fragile "nudge units" that disappear with the next budget cut. Let's be honest: if behavioral science only works in stable, well-funded Western bureaucracies, then it's not nearly as powerful as we claim. And the evidence already contradicts that narrative. From Busara to ideas42, J-PAL, and the Inter-American Development Bank, behavioral approaches have been delivering results across the Global South for years. The problem isn't applicability — it's where we choose to look, invest, and publish. That's why Central and Eastern Europe (CEE) matters. Not as a "new market" for behavioral policy — but as a proving ground. If an intervention can survive weak institutions, political volatility, and constant information manipulation, then it's genuinely robust. If it can't, we need to stop overselling it. Behavioral public policy doesn't need more validation in comfortable settings. It needs to prove it works where things are messy. That's the real test — and we're not paying enough attention to it.

- https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-economics/articles/10.3389/frbhe.2026.1813982/full
- Autres publications de l'auteur (Banerjee, notamment « past, present & future » 2025) : https://www.kcl.ac.uk/people/sanchayan-banerjee

### @@26 — 1 million d'actions loguées dans l'app AWorld (ONU) ⭐
> In our new paper, we collaborated with AWorld, the United Nations' official platform for personal action on climate change and sustainability. We analyze more than 1 million logged actions from 4,369 users to examine behavioral patterns, data quality, and links to carbon footprints and psychological factors.
>
> **1. Diverse usage patterns.** App use varies dramatically. Many users engage briefly and log few actions, while a smaller group contributes most of the data. Even among active users, reporting is intermittent, with gaps and short bursts of activity. This makes it difficult to determine whether missing data indicates no action or simply no reporting.
>
> **2. A focus on low-impact, repeatable actions.** Users predominantly report actions that can be performed frequently, such as turning off lights or walking. These actions are easy to log and often align with app design and engagement features. However, they typically have limited potential for emissions reduction, except for food, where repeated actions such as eating vegetarian meals can meaningfully reduce emissions over time.
>
> **3. Weak link to carbon footprints.** We find no strong relationship between the number of reported actions and users' overall carbon footprints, except in the food domain. One reason is that many low-impact actions can be performed alongside high-impact behaviors. In domains like mobility, someone can frequently "take the train instead of the plane" and still fly often.
>
> **4. Limited association with psychological variables.** Based on a subsample of users who answered a survey, we find little evidence that environmental identity, perceived effectiveness of individual action, or climate concern in one's inner circle predict the number of reported actions. This may reflect a combination of measurement challenges, selection into app use, ceiling effects, and the fact that many reported actions are low-cost and low-impact.

- [ ] https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/2515-7620/ae5b4c

**→ Papier important pour le bilan négatif.** Il documente le mécanisme précis par lequel l'action individuelle mesurée se déconnecte de l'impact : les gestes faciles à loguer sont ceux qui comptent le moins, et ils coexistent avec les comportements à fort impact. C'est l'illustration empirique de la critique des écogestes (@@37).

### @@47 / @@55 — *Intervention ensembles* : combiner plutôt que chercher le meilleur mécanisme ⭐
> Behavioral science often asks: What is the single best theory of change for a particular behavior? But for many of the policy contexts we care about, from climate change to democratic values, the best motivational lever may not be an isolated mechanism. In our newest research led by Anandita Sabherwal, Danielle Goldwert and Elke Weber, we introduce **intervention ensembles**: strategic combinations of psychological modules designed to target complementary barriers to action. Across a new megastudy and a follow-up preregistered experiment (N = 11,035), we test ensembles built in three ways: 1) combining previously top-performing interventions 2) covering key psychological mechanisms 3) aligning individual-, collective-, and system-level processes.
>
> Here's what we find: Ensembles consistently outperform their constituent single-mechanism interventions across outcomes, including climate policy support, real donation behavior, and reductions in partisan animosity. The strongest approach is a **multi-level ensemble** that combines individual-level processes, collective-level processes, and system-level processes (specifically, psychological distance, social norms, and governmental efficacy). Critically, **this is not just "more is better."** The most effective ensembles are not the longest or most content-heavy. They are smaller, coordinated combinations that align personal relevance, social influence, and institutional efficacy. Additional analyses suggest that ensembles work by resolving complementary psychological barriers, not by giving people more arguments. Overall, for complex societal challenges, behavioral science may need to move beyond the search for the single best mechanism and toward the design of coordinated intervention systems.

- [ ] https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/8j7n2_v1

**→ Contre-proposition la plus solide empiriquement du lot.** Elle répond directement au résultat décevant de @@27 : si aucune caractéristique de forme ne prédit l'efficacité, c'est peut-être la *combinaison* qui est le bon niveau d'analyse. À lire en regard du Nature Energy du 18/08, qui trouve la même chose côté policy mix.

### @@15 — SCOPE (Airbel / IRC) : la science comportementale pour les contextes fragiles ⭐
> SCOPE: a behavioral science approach we've developed and tested in some of the world's most complex settings, from Niger to South Sudan and beyond. This approach isn't about tweaking defaults or optimizing decisions. SCOPE is built for wicked challenges, like climate adaptation in fragile states or food insecurity in conflict zones, where problems are layered, shifting, and often hard to define. We developed it not in theory, but through fieldwork, iteration, and deep interdisciplinary collaboration. More than a toolkit, SCOPE is a rethink of how we apply behavioral science in complexity, blending mindset and method across five key shifts:
> - From isolated behaviors → to interactions in systems and relationships
> - From predefined problems → to open exploration, shaped with communities
> - From linear methods → to adaptive, interdisciplinary approaches
> - From top-down solutions → to community-led design and iteration
> - From only what's measurable → to what's meaningful and transformative to those closest to the issue
>
> SCOPE is still evolving. It's a living methodology we'll keep adapting and improving through practice.

- [ ] https://www.linkedin.com/posts/britt-titus-a25b0253_summary-of-scope-activity-7420460983615127552--Qjz

## Lecture d'ensemble

Ce versement n'est pas une bibliographie neutre : c'est **la matière première de la Partie 1**, déposée d'un coup. Trois observations utiles pour la suite.

**1. Le corpus est déjà organisé selon le plan de la revue, sans le dire.** Les entrées se répartissent presque naturellement dans les six cases — avec un déséquilibre net : les *contre-propositions* (14 entrées) et les *critiques/épistémologie* (13) pèsent plus lourd que le *bilan dans les transitions* (10). Le plan dit « rendre les critiques constructives » ; le corpus est déjà majoritairement du côté des contre-propositions. **Le risque est de sous-documenter le bilan empirique lui-même**, qui est pourtant ce qui donne autorité à la critique.

**2. Un motif revient trois fois de façon indépendante : le problème est terminologique avant d'être empirique.** @@54 (glossaire OMS, consensus Delphi), @@39 (taxonomie éthique, « conceptual infrastructure quietly broken ») et la défragmentation de la psychologie ([[2026-07-16]]) disent la même chose depuis trois positions différentes. Ce n'est probablement pas un hasard et mérite un traitement explicite dans la sous-section « différentes approches ».

**3. Les ressources les plus originales sont les moins académiques.** Pedwell sur l'habitude (@@43), l'ethnographie des déchets (@@56), les *stories of change* (@@50) et le banc-stop (@@38) sont ce que le corpus a de plus distinctif — et ce qu'on ne trouvera pas dans une revue standard des *behavioral insights*. Ce sont eux qui alimentent le « ce que cela dessine en creux ».

## Limites de cette capture

- Textes majoritairement **repris de posts LinkedIn de leurs auteurs** : ce sont des résumés promotionnels, pas des abstracts revus. À vérifier contre les papiers avant toute citation.
- Plusieurs liens sont des **redirections LinkedIn** ou des pages d'accueil de revue (@@56 pointe vers `ethnographiques.org` sans article précis, @@20 vers un `doiLanding` sans DOI) : à résoudre.
- Les entrées marquées ⭐ sont celles qui justifient une note de papier à part entière dans `00_notes_papers/`.

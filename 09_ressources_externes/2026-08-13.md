# Objectifs auto-choisis vs. assignés — l'autonomie comme levier

## Contenu du message

The idea behind the paper is simple: When we want people to change their behavior — exercise more, save more, or spend less time on their phones — we often assign them a goal (10,000 steps, 30 minutes of exercise, 20% less screen time).

But what if, instead of telling people what their goal should be, we let them choose it themselves? Can a small amount of autonomy make behavior change more effective?

**What did we find?**

During the five-week intervention, participants who chose their own goals reduced their phone use by 26 minutes more per day than those who were assigned a goal — a 73% larger reduction.

They also achieved their daily goals 11 percentage points more often.

One detail I found particularly interesting: choosing the easiest goal maximized the chance of earning the daily incentive, yet about one-quarter of participants still voluntarily chose the more ambitious 20% or 30% goals (may be as commitment).

Reductions in phone use and higher goal achievement were associated with improvements in perceived addiction, depressive, and anxiety symptoms.

The broader takeaway is simple: choice itself can matter. Giving people an opportunity to choose their own goals may increase ownership and engagement and ultimately make behavior change more effective.

This relatively small design change can be incorporated into digital-health tools, workplace wellness programs, and other interventions that rely on goal setting.

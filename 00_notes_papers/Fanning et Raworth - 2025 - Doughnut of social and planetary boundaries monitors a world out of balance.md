# Abstract 
The [[The Doughnut framework]] doughnut-shaped framework of social and planetary boundaries (the 'Doughnut') provides a concise visual assessment of progress towards the goal of meeting the needs of all people within the means of the living planet. Here we present a renewed Doughnut framework with a revised set of 35 indicators that monitor trends in social deprivation and ecological overshoot over the 2000–2022 period. Although global gross domestic product (GDP) has more than doubled, our median results show a modest achievement in reducing human deprivation that would have to accelerate fivefold to meet the needs of all people by 2030. Meanwhile, the increase in ecological overshoot would have to stop immediately and accelerate nearly two times faster towards planetary boundaries to safeguard Earth-system stability by 2050. Disaggregating these global findings shows that the richest 20% of nations, with 15% of the global population, contribute more than 40% of annual ecological overshoot, whereas the poorest 40% of countries, with 42% of the global population, experience more than 60% of the social shortfall. These trends and inequalities reaffirm the case for overcoming the dependence of nations on perpetual GDP growth and reorienting towards regenerative and distributive economic activity—within and between nations—that assigns priority to human needs and planetary integrity. 

# Key claims / thesis
- Present a strengthened framework of boundaries and indicators for the Doughnut at the global scale, bringing together recent advances in planetary boundaries science and in the availability of data monitoring social outcomes worldwide.
- Explore inequalities that tend to be masked by such global aggregates by comparing the performance of countries clustered by income level, using a comparable set of social indicators coupled with recent estimates of 'downscaled' planetary boundaries.
- Argue that GDP growth has failed to bring humanity into the Doughnut: social improvement is too slow and ecological overshoot is accelerating. The case for reorienting towards regenerative and distributive economic activity is reaffirmed.

# Methods
- **35 indicators** across 21 social dimensions and 14 ecological dimensions, covering the period **2000–2022** (time-series data available for N=30 indicators).
- Social shortfall measured as the share of global population falling below minimum standards in the Doughnut's social foundation. Ecological overshoot measured as % beyond the respective planetary boundary.
- **Historical trends** estimated by ordinary least squares (OLS) regression; reported as annual percentage-point change. Two-sided hypothesis tests with significance at 99% (*) and 99.9% (**).
- **Scenario trends** (to eliminate shortfall by 2030 / overshoot by 2050) calculated as the annual % change required from 2022 levels to zero.
- **Country-cluster analysis**: 193 countries grouped into three income clusters (poorest 40%, middle 40%, richest 20%) by average national income per capita 2000–2022.
- Ecological footprints for cross-country comparison: consumption-based data for 168 countries in 2017, covering 6 indicators (CO₂, nitrogen, phosphorus, blue water, species loss, HANPP). Compared against equality-based per capita downscaled planetary boundaries.

# Main findings
- Global GDP more than doubled 2000–2022, yet median social shortfall only fell from 47% to 35% of humanity — a pace that would need to accelerate **~5× to meet all social needs by 2030**
- The **5 largest social improvements** (2000–2022): internet connectivity, health services coverage, child survival, safe sanitation, clean indoor fuels (24–56% of humanity escaping shortfall in these areas).
- **5 social indicators show little or worsening trends**: food insecurity and autocratic regimes are significantly worsening; youth unemployment, lack of social support, and perceptions of corruption show no meaningful improvement.
- Ecological overshoot worsened across **9 of 10** indicators with available time-series data. Median overshoot level rose from 75% to 96% above planetary boundaries. Chemical pollution and phosphorus overshoot are the most severe (>3,000% above boundary).
- To eliminate ecological overshoot by 2050, current trends would need to **reverse immediately and accelerate nearly 2× faster** (median: 6.9 %pt/year required).
- **Country-cluster inequalities**: the richest 20% of nations (15% of world population) contribute >40% of annual ecological overshoot; the poorest 40% of countries (42% of world population) experience >60% of global social shortfall.
- The richest 20% cluster's per capita environmental footprints are 1.3–12.4× larger than those of the poorest 40% cluster.
- Ocean acidification is the only ecological indicator still within its boundary as of 2022, but is closing rapidly.
- Ozone layer depletion is the only indicator showing no change (essentially stable and near boundary).

# Connections
- [[The Doughnut framework]] : this paper is the third iteration and first systematic annual monitor of the Doughnut (following Raworth 2012 and 2017).
- [[Planetary boundaries]] : directly builds on Rockström et al. (2009) and Steffen et al. (2015); incorporates the updated Rockström et al. (2023) boundary science.
- SDGs : the Doughnut offers a complementary but more holistic dashboard (35 indicators vs. 169 SDG indicators), with a more balanced social–ecological framing.
- Post-growth / degrowth literature (Hickel et al., Jackson, O'Neill et al.) — paper explicitly supports moving beyond GDP dependence.
- Downscaled planetary boundaries (Turner & Willis 2022) : used for the country-cluster ecological footprint comparison.
- Doughnut Economics Action Lab (DEAL) : institutional context; over 50 city and district governments have embedded the Doughnut since 2019.
- Ecological macroeconomic modelling : the data are positioned as an input for models assessing decoupling of wellbeing from ecological overshoot.

# My notes / critique
- The move from a static snapshot to a **time-series monitor** is the key methodological advance — it makes trajectory visible, not just position.
- The paper is unusually direct in its policy conclusion: GDP growth is not solving the problem and may be structurally incompatible with staying in the Doughnut.
- **Aggregation problem**: global and even cluster-level averages mask enormous within-country inequality. The authors acknowledge this, but it limits actionability.
- The planetary boundaries framework itself remains contested (ref. 26 in the paper); some boundaries (chemical pollution, biodiversity) have wide uncertainty ranges, which affects the % overshoot numbers.
- "Humanity" as a single entity is a simplification — the paper is aware of this but the global Doughnut still risks naturalising an abstraction that serves richer countries.
- The use of **consumption-based footprints** for the cross-country comparison is methodologically important and more honest about responsibility than production-based measures — worth flagging in any policy use.
- The 5× acceleration needed for social goals vs. 2× for ecological goals by the respective deadlines (2030 vs. 2050) creates a perverse asymmetry: the social challenge is proportionally harder on a shorter timeline.
- No causal mechanism is modelled — the paper is descriptive/diagnostic, not predictive.
- This paper links the ecological transformation to social justice. this is a general aspect of the [[The Doughnut framework]] but this paper goes further with the decomposition of countries regarding their wealth.
